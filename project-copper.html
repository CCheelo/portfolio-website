Copper Price Forecasting

2024–2025 · Academic Project R · ARMA · GARCH · Time Series

Time-series modelling of daily copper prices (USD/tonne, 2000–2025) — a critical input for Zambia's economy. Following the Box–Jenkins workflow, the ACF/PACF and BIC select an AR(2) model for returns; the analysis then extends it to an ARMA–GARCH model that captures the volatility clustering the AR(2) misses.

Full Report (PDF) GitHub Code Summary Overview

Summary Overview

Research Question

Can past copper prices predict future ones? Copper is the backbone of Zambia's economy, so the predictability of its price feeds directly into the national budget, the exchange rate, and mining-sector planning. This project models 6,298 daily copper prices (USD/tonne, Dec 2000 – May 2025) to find out.

Data & Methodology

Daily copper spot prices (LME-style, USD/tonne). The price level is non-stationary, so the analysis works with log returns and follows the Box–Jenkins workflow:

  • → Stationarity testing — ADF and KPSS on the level and on returns
  • → ACF/PACF + BIC — a grid search selects an AR(2) model
  • → Diagnostics — Ljung–Box, ARCH-LM, and Jarque–Bera on the residuals
  • → Out-of-sample backtest — one-step forecasts vs a random walk (RMSE/MAE)
  • → ARMA–GARCH extension — GARCH(1,1) with Student-t innovations

Original Model — AR(2)

Both the ACF and PACF of returns spike at lags 1–2 then cut off, and a BIC grid search (confirmed by auto.arima) lands on ARMA(2,0). Both autoregressive terms are highly significant. But the model is only valid in the mean: out of sample it does not beat a naive random walk (RMSE 118.3 vs 117.7), calling the next day's direction correctly just 48.8% of the time. Copper, it turns out, is close to weak-form efficient — its price level is essentially unforecastable.

Improved Model — ARMA–GARCH

The AR(2) residuals fail decisively on volatility clustering (ARCH-LM p ≈ 0) and heavy tails. Extending the model to ARMA(2,0)–GARCH(1,1) with Student-t innovations slashes the BIC from 24,742 to 21,872, eliminates the ARCH from the standardised residuals, and recovers the stylised facts of commodity returns: volatility persistence of 0.97 and tails near 4.6 degrees of freedom.

Why it's an improvement: the GARCH model gives valid standard errors, time-varying (honest) forecast intervals, and accurate tail-risk / value-at-risk — even though it doesn't make the price more predictable in the mean. The key insight: for copper, the direction isn't predictable, but the risk is — and the risk is what's worth modelling.

Why It Matters

For a copper-dependent economy, knowing that the price level follows a near-random walk while its volatility is persistent has real implications: forecasting effort is better spent on risk and scenario analysis than on chasing point forecasts, and the persistence of volatility shocks strengthens the case for fiscal stabilisation buffers.
